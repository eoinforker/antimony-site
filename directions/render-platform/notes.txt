Platform, path traced

What this is
  platform_scene.py builds the Platform scene procedurally and renders it with Cycles. The world
  matches the real-time version: the far wall across the tracks, a 4.98 by 2.8 lightbox with its
  foot 0.35 above platform level, the vault springing at 3.7 as a half ellipse (6 across, 2.4 up),
  the platform edge 3 m from the far wall, the track bed 1.05 below, the camera at eye height
  9.5 m back with a vertical field of 40 degrees and 1.5 degrees of yaw.
  Glazed subway tile in brick bond (per-tile tilt, bevelled edges, a glaze coat, soot, leaks and
  grimy grout), a Prussian tile band above and below the box, cast iron ribs every five metres,
  a cornice with tube lamps (the right-hand one failing and dimmer, two dead ones over the box,
  more off frame behind the camera), sleepers, running and conductor rails on their pots, a wet
  slab floor with standing water, a coated figure standing still in the print's light, drips
  falling from the vault, a little haze.
  The only image is the print: Antimony's Grow page (directions/grow/shots/rest-desktop.png),
  cropped to 16:9, with the Break mark redrawn from its exact geometry (the Grow shot has a blade
  of grass at the foot of the seam; the redraw removes it, so nothing grows on the mark).

  Blender 4.5.15 LTS, Cycles on CPU (4 threads), OpenImageDenoise with albedo and normal,
  adaptive sampling, light tree, AgX view (Base Contrast), a soft bloom.

  Two deliberate cheats, both common on film sets:
  - The print is a practical on a dimmer: the camera and mirror reflections see it at a level
    that keeps the print readable, while the light it throws on tiles, floor and figure is
    2.2 times brighter (camera rays versus shadow and diffuse rays).
  - The haze scatters camera rays only. It is seen but not bounced through, which roughly halves
    the cost of the volume on CPU.

  Run: blender -b -P platform_scene.py -- --variant night        (or overgrown)
  The loop (see below):
    --variant loop --pass on   --out on.exr  --samples 160
    --variant loop --pass off  --out off.exr --samples 160
    --variant loop --pass anim --out anim_####.exr --samples 48 --frames 1-72
    --variant loop --pass compose --src <folder of the EXRs> --out f_####.png --frames 1-72
  then ffmpeg (24 fps, H.264 CRF 17 and VP9 CRF 30).

Render times, 4 CPU threads, no GPU
  night.png      1920 x 1200   1393 s (23 min 13 s), about 98 samples, stopped by the 23 minute limit
  overgrown.png  1920 x 1200   1400 s (23 min 20 s), about 98 samples, stopped by the 23 minute limit
  night-loop     1280 x 800, 72 frames at 24 fps (3 s), seamless
                 lamp on pass 431 s, lamp off pass 421 s (160 samples each),
                 moving water 72 frames at about 15 s each (18 min), compose 42 s, encode seconds
                 total about 33 minutes
  Part of the session shared the machine with other jobs; an early night render under that load
  reached only about 40 samples in the same 23 minutes.

How the loop is made, and its limits
  Light adds linearly, so the failing lamp's flicker is exact as off + v(t) (on minus off),
  from two still passes in linear EXR. The drips (real objects with motion blur) and the rings
  they leave in the puddles are rendered every frame on their own, with the rest of the station
  set to indirect only, so each drip refracts and reflects the real scene, then laid over the
  stills and taken through the same AgX view. Every motion repeats on a 24, 36 or 72 frame
  period, so frame 72 runs straight into frame 1.
  Not in this loop: haze drift (the haze is static; drifting haze changes every pixel and needs
  full renders per frame), and the drips do not cast light or shadow onto the scene.

What a 1920 x 1080, 8 second loop would cost (192 frames at 24 fps)
  The same pass method (static light, a flickering lamp, moving water):
    two still passes at 1920 x 1080 (about 2 x 7.2 min x 2.0 for the pixels) about 30 min
    192 water frames at about 30 s                                            about 1.6 h
    total on this machine                                                     about 2 to 2.5 hours
  Full renders of every frame (needed for drifting haze, swaying grass in the overgrown version,
  any camera move): about 21 min a frame at the quality of the stills, so
    192 x 21 min                                                              about 67 hours
  On one current desktop GPU the full renders would be roughly 1 to 2 min a frame, about 3 to 6
  hours; a small render farm brings it under an hour.

Overgrown, and one honest limit
  The vault has opened near the far side, left of the box (a broken grating, sky visible at the
  top of the frame) and a newer glazed skylight runs along the crown out of frame; a shaft of
  daylight falls through the opening into the track bed with dust and pollen held in it. Tall
  grass and ferns fill the track bed and rise above the platform edge as a strip of real meadow
  under the printed one; tufts grow from the platform joints and the tile joints (never within
  a hand's width of the box), moss in the grout, ivy trailing from the cornice and the opening.
  Puddles full of sky: with the real-time framing, the floor in frame can only mirror the far
  wall below about 2 m, never the opening or the skylight, so the puddles mirror the print, the
  meadow in the track bed and the lit tiles rather than sky. A lower or tilted camera would be
  needed to put sky in them.
